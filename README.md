⛅ # TexasClimate: Weather, Energy, and Switchgrass Biofuel Intelligence

**TexasClimate** is a student-built environmental intelligence platform that connects Texas weather, agriculture, energy demand, fuel use, and switchgrass biofuel research in one accessible dashboard.

Live site: https://haohanzang-ai.github.io/Weather-Channel/
Repository: https://github.com/haohanzang-ai/Weather-Channel
Status: Educational prototype
Platform: GitHub Pages
Technologies: HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Chart.js

---

## Project Overview

Most weather websites focus on immediate conditions: today’s temperature, tomorrow’s rain, or the chance of a storm. TexasClimate begins with that familiar information, but extends the question further.

In Texas, weather affects more than daily plans. Heat waves increase electricity demand. Drought places pressure on crops, reservoirs, and irrigation. Fuel prices affect households and transportation. Agriculture, climate, energy, and fuel use are connected, but those connections are not always easy to see in a standard forecast.

TexasClimate was created to make those relationships more visible.

The project uses a public-facing dashboard to connect local environmental conditions with a broader scientific question:

> How can Texas weather, agriculture, energy demand, and fuel use help the public understand the potential and limitations of switchgrass as a future biofuel source?

Switchgrass (*Panicum virgatum*) is a native North American perennial grass that has been studied as a possible cellulosic biofuel feedstock. It is promising because it is non-food biomass and may have long-term value in renewable fuel systems. At the same time, it is not a simple or immediate fuel solution. The main challenge is converting its tough plant cell wall into usable fuel at industrial scale.

This project presents both sides: the opportunity of switchgrass and the difficulty of making it practical.

---

## Mission

The mission of TexasClimate is to make climate, agriculture, energy, and biofuel science easier to understand through data visualization and plain-language explanation.

The platform is designed around five guiding questions:

1. How does Texas weather affect agriculture, water, and energy demand?
2. Why do heat, drought, fuel use, and climate stress matter together?
3. What is switchgrass, and why has it been studied as a biofuel crop?
4. Why is lignin one of the major barriers to cellulosic biofuel production?
5. How can a public dashboard help students and everyday users understand environmental systems?

TexasClimate is not intended to be an official forecasting system, emergency tool, or commercial biofuel model. It is an educational project designed to make complex environmental and energy topics easier to explore.

## Why Switchgrass?

Switchgrass is the research focus of the project because it connects plant biology, agriculture, renewable energy, and climate resilience.

Unlike corn ethanol, switchgrass is not a food crop. It is a perennial biomass crop that has been studied for possible use in cellulosic ethanol and other bioenergy pathways. In theory, plant biomass can be broken down into sugars and converted into fuels or bioproducts.

The difficulty is that switchgrass is not easy to process.

Its cell wall contains cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Cellulose and hemicellulose contain sugars that can potentially be converted into fuel. Lignin gives the plant strength and protection, but it also blocks access to those sugars. This makes conversion more expensive and technically difficult.

That challenge is the central reason switchgrass is scientifically important but not yet widely used as a mass-produced fuel source.

TexasClimate uses switchgrass as a case study because it shows how renewable energy questions are rarely simple. A crop can be promising in research, but still face major barriers in processing, infrastructure, cost, and scale.

---

## The Biofuel Conversion Problem

The simplified pathway from switchgrass to fuel is:

```text
Sunlight → Switchgrass biomass → Pretreatment → Cell wall breakdown → Sugars → Fermentation or conversion → Biofuel
```

The hardest step is breaking down the plant cell wall.

This matters because:

* **Cellulose** can be broken down into sugars.
* **Hemicellulose** can also contribute usable sugars.
* **Lignin** protects the plant and limits access to those sugars.
* Pretreatment and enzyme conversion can be expensive.
* Industrial-scale production requires lower costs, better efficiency, and reliable processing methods.

The project focuses on lignin and cellulose because that is where the real scientific challenge begins. It is not enough to say that grass can become fuel. The important question is whether the conversion process can become efficient, affordable, and scalable.

## Gas Prices and Fuel Awareness

TexasClimate does not claim that switchgrass will directly lower gasoline prices.

Instead, the fuel section helps users think about energy use and fuel diversification. Gasoline is familiar to most households, so it provides a practical starting point for understanding larger energy questions.

The project uses fuel-use scenarios to help users explore questions such as:

* How much gasoline does a household use each week?
* How do changes in fuel prices affect everyday costs?
* Why are alternative fuels studied?
* What does fuel diversification mean?
* Where could cellulosic biofuels fit into a broader energy future?

Any calculator or replacement scenario on the website is educational. It is not a prediction of real savings, market prices, or commercial switchgrass availability.

## Data and Accuracy Statement

TexasClimate is currently an educational prototype.

Some values may be sample data, built-in demonstration data, or illustrative estimates. These values are included to show how a climate, agriculture, energy, and biofuel dashboard could work.

The website should not be used for:

* emergency weather decisions
* farming or irrigation decisions
* investment decisions
* fuel market predictions
* public policy decisions
* operational grid planning
* disaster response

For official real-time information, users should rely on sources such as the National Weather Service, NOAA, EPA/AirNow, ERCOT, the U.S. Drought Monitor, Water Data for Texas, and local emergency management agencies.

## AI-Assisted Interpretation

TexasClimate includes AI-assisted and rule-based interpretation tools that summarize environmental patterns and explain them in plain language.

These features are designed for education. They are not official forecasts, scientific guarantees, emergency alerts, or professional recommendations.

The goal is to help users ask better questions, compare related environmental factors, and understand how weather, agriculture, energy, and biofuel research connect.

## Current Limitations

TexasClimate is still under active development. The main limitations are:

* some data is sample or demonstration data
* several modules still need official public API connections
* the switchgrass model needs more peer-reviewed support
* the fuel calculator is educational rather than predictive
* the site needs more user testing and feedback
* mobile layout and accessibility can still be improved

These limitations are documented because credibility is part of the project. The goal is not to make the prototype seem more advanced than it is, but to build it into a stronger and more useful resource over time.
